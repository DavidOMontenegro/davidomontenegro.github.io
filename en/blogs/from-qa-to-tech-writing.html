From QA to Tech Writing

Not long after joining, I was offered a new opportunity: moving into the Technical Writing team to help reduce a growing backlog of documentation. The company went through some restructuring that led to the QA department being shut down. Fortunately for me, I was already recognized as a skilled writer and was spared from the layoffs.


It was a big shift from writing automated tests in Poshi to writing documentation in plain old English, but ironically, this actually got me a lot closer to the engineering work I was chasing. Now, instead of existing in an isolated environment, I was actively participating in sprint plannings and product reviews along with the engineers and product owners. I also got to put my programming knowledge to use - explaining code, writing snippets, and teaching developers.


It didn't take too long to notice why I was migrated to documentation rather than development. The writing team had been understaffed for years and was facing two significant issues: new products requiring brand new documentation and a growing backlog with forgotten work. Within two months of becoming an Associate Technical Writer, I became the head writer for three different components, a level of responsibility my manager confessed he usually reserves for more experienced team members. It was a busy year and I appreciated the trust placed in me, especially since I got to document Liferay's core infrastructure and cloud platform: the two areas I was most experienced in.


If you can't see how tech writing could possibly be closer to development than automated testing, let me enlighten you. Liferay's fundamental architecture is built on OSGi modules. It's possible to extend and customize by building your own custom modules that implement and override various features. With Liferay being an open source product and having these tech-intensive code-level extension capabilities, someone needed to document them all. That's where I came in. I got to write several modules demonstrating different functionalities, but you'll read more about them in a future post solely about all the development experience I gained. (It's pretty cool stuff)


I'll admit I was a bit disappointed at first. I never saw myself as a writer, always an engineer. But as time passed, I realized this work actually aligns a lot more with my strengths than typical development. While I understand code and can write it pretty well, my favorite part about software development is solving other people's problems. Technical writing is one of the few areas in software where my engineering knowledge can be put to use outside of engineering. This ability to understand highly technical concepts and teach others in a language they can actually understand is fundamental to Developer Relations and the key to the success I've experienced so far in my career.


Working independently on these areas also taught me how to organize myself and prioritize my tasks. I learned these soft skills are just as important as the technical ones, but you'll read more about this in the next posts too. Stay tuned!
